Biosecurity in Site Inspections

Biosecurity protects Tasmania's unique ecosystems from invasive species, pests, and diseases.

What is Biosecurity?

Biosecurity refers to measures taken to prevent the introduction and spread of harmful organisms that can threaten the environment, agriculture, and human health.

Tasmania's isolation makes it particularly vulnerable to biosecurity threats. Once established, many invasive species are extremely difficult to control or eradicate.

Key Biosecurity Threats in Tasmania

Phytophthora cinnamomi

A soil-borne fungus that causes root rot in many native species. Spreads through soil movement, water, and infected plant material.

Affects: Xanthorrhoea, Banksia, Epacris, and many other native plants

Weeds of National Significance

Invasive plants that outcompete natives and degrade habitats.

Key species: Gorse (Ulex europaeus), Blackberry (Rubus fruticosus), Spanish Heath (Erica lusitanica)

Feral Animals

Introduced animals that prey on native wildlife or compete for resources.

Key species: Feral cats, foxes, rabbits, deer, pigs

Plant & Animal Diseases

Pathogens that threaten native species and agricultural production.

Examples: Myrtle wilt, Xylella, Devil Facial Tumor Disease

Hygiene Practices

Implementing good hygiene practices is essential to prevent the spread of biosecurity threats.

Vehicles

Clean mud and plant material from wheels and underbody before entering and leaving sites.

Footwear

Scrub boots with a stiff brush and disinfect with suitable solution (e.g., F10, methylated spirits).

Equipment

Clean all tools and equipment between sites. Pay special attention to soil-contaminated items.

Clothing

Brush off seeds and plant fragments. Consider changing clothes between high-risk sites.

In the Field

Always follow the "Check-in, Check-out" protocol: wash and disinfect vehicles and equipment before entering a site, and repeat when leaving. This is especially important in areas with known Phytophthora presence.

Reporting Biosecurity Hazards

If you identify a biosecurity threat during an inspection:

1

Document the finding

Take photos, note GPS coordinates, record species/type, estimate extent.

2

Report immediately

Contact Biosecurity Tasmania, NRE Tasmania, or the relevant authority.

3

Implement hygiene measures

Ensure no further spread occurs through cleaning and containment.

4

Follow up

Provide additional information as requested and support management actions.

Abiotic Elements

Understanding the physical environment is essential for interpreting ecological patterns and making informed management decisions.

Soil

Soil is the foundation of terrestrial ecosystems. Tasmania has diverse soil types including dermosols, kurosols, sodosols, and organosols.

Soil Properties to Assess

  • pH: Affects nutrient availability and plant selection
  • Nutrients: Nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium levels
  • Organic matter: Indicates soil health and biological activity
  • Texture: Sand, silt, clay proportions
  • Structure: How particles clump together
  • Compaction: Indicates disturbance or heavy use
  • Salinity: Salt levels affect plant growth

How to Assess Soil in the Field

  • Texture: Feel test - rub soil between fingers
  • pH: Use a field pH test kit
  • Compaction: Push a soil penetrometer or use a spade
  • Organic matter: Observe color and feel
  • Erosion: Look for rills, gullies, exposed roots
  • Salinity: Look for salt crusts or salt-tolerant species

TAS Example: Midlands Soils

Dolerite-derived soils in the Midlands are naturally low in phosphorus and nitrogen, supporting distinctive grassy woodlands. Understanding this helps explain why native grassland species are adapted to low nutrient conditions, and why nutrient enrichment from agricultural runoff can promote weed invasion.

Climate & Weather

Climate influences which species can survive and how ecosystems function.

Key Climate Factors

  • Annual rainfall: Varies from 500mm (east) to 3000mm+ (west)
  • Temperature: Maritime climate with distinct seasons
  • Frost: Frequency and severity
  • Snow: Alpine areas experience regular snowfall
  • Wind: Roaring Forties affect western and southern coasts
  • Climate change: Drying trends, increased fire risk, sea level rise

How to Record Climate on Site

  • Temperature: Use a digital thermometer
  • Rainfall: Check BOM data for nearest station
  • Aspect: North-facing slopes are warmer and drier
  • Elevation: Higher altitudes are cooler and wetter
  • Wind exposure: Assess vegetation for wind pruning or salt spray
  • Frost hollows: Cold air drainage areas

Tip: Always record weather conditions during your inspection - this helps interpret your observations and provides context for future monitoring.

Hydrology & Drainage

Water movement shapes landscapes and determines which ecosystems can exist.

Hydrological Features to Observe

  • Gradient and slope: Steeper slopes increase runoff and erosion risk
  • Drainage patterns: Well-drained, waterlogged, or seasonal wetlands
  • Base level: Water table depth and fluctuations
  • Water quality: Turbidity, salinity, pH, nutrients
  • Erosion: Gullies, rills, bank slumping

How to Assess Hydrology

  • Soil moisture: Feel test or use a moisture meter
  • Water table: Look for wetland indicator species
  • Runoff: Observe drainage lines and erosion patterns
  • Water quality: Use field test kits (pH, turbidity, salinity)
  • Flooding: Look for debris lines, water marks on trees

Geology & Landform

Geology and landform shape soil types, drainage patterns, and vegetation communities.

Key Rock Types

  • Dolerite: Extensive throughout Tasmania, forms nutrient-poor soils
  • Granite: Produces sandy, well-drained soils
  • Sandstone: Forms low-nutrient, erodible soils
  • Limestone: Calcareous, supports distinctive vegetation
  • Basalt: Produces fertile, clay-rich soils

Landform Features

  • Mountains: Alpine ecosystems, cold and wet
  • Valleys: Fertile alluvial soils
  • Plains: Grasslands and woodlands
  • Coastlines: Dunes, saltmarshes, heathlands

Erosion Processes

  • Water erosion: Sheet, rill, gully, streambank
  • Wind erosion: Coastal and dry areas
  • Chemical erosion: Soil degradation, acidification
  • Mass movement: Landslides, slumping

Topography

  • Aspect: North vs south facing slopes
  • Elevation: Affects temperature and rainfall
  • Slope steepness: Affects erosion and accessibility
  • Position: Ridge, mid-slope, valley floor

Microorganisms: The Hidden Workforce

Microorganisms are the unseen engine of ecosystem function, driving nutrient cycling, soil formation, and plant health.

Mycorrhizal Fungi

Mycorrhizal fungi form symbiotic relationships with plant roots, extending the plant's root system and improving access to water and nutrients.

Why They Matter

  • Most Tasmanian native plants rely on mycorrhizal associations
  • Essential for nutrient uptake (especially phosphorus)
  • Improve drought tolerance
  • Protect against soil pathogens

What to Observe

  • Fungal hyphae in the soil (white threads)
  • Mushrooms or fruiting bodies in the litter
  • Healthy root systems with fine mycorrhizal networks
  • Ectomycorrhizal associations on tree roots

Decomposers & Nutrient Cycling

Bacteria and fungi break down organic matter, recycling nutrients back into the soil.

  • Leaf litter breakdown: Essential for soil formation and nutrient cycling
  • Humus formation: Decomposed organic matter improves soil structure
  • Nutrient release: Nitrogen, phosphorus, and other nutrients become available to plants
  • Carbon cycling: Decomposition releases carbon dioxide, but some carbon is stored in soil

Nitrogen Fixers

Nitrogen-fixing bacteria convert atmospheric nitrogen into plant-available forms.

  • Rhizobium bacteria: Form nodules on legume roots (e.g., Acacia, Hovea)
  • Frankia bacteria: Associate with non-legumes like She-oaks (Allocasuarina)
  • Free-living bacteria: Fix nitrogen in soil without plant hosts
  • Essential for: Soil fertility, especially in nutrient-poor Tasmanian soils

Pathogens & Disease

Some microorganisms cause disease and can devastate ecosystems.

Key Pathogens in Tasmania

  • Phytophthora cinnamomi: Root rot affecting over 100 species
  • Myrtle wilt: Fungal disease affecting Myrtle Beech
  • Xylella: Bacterial disease affecting many plants
  • Devil Facial Tumor Disease: Transmissible cancer

Identifying Disease in the Field

  • Dieback: Dead or dying branches
  • Discoloration: Unusual leaf colors (yellow, brown, red)
  • Lesions: Sunken, discolored areas on bark
  • Wilting: Drooping leaves despite adequate water
  • Fungal growth: Mushrooms, brackets, mycelium

In the Field

Look for signs of microbial activity: healthy leaf litter decomposition, fungal hyphae in soil, and healthy root systems. These indicate functioning soil ecosystems with active microbial communities.

Ecosystem Services

Ecosystem services are the benefits that people obtain from ecosystems. Understanding these helps justify conservation and inform management decisions.

Provisioning Services

Products obtained directly from ecosystems:

  • Timber and wood products: Forestry provides renewable resources
  • Food: Fish, game, native bush tucker (e.g., Murnong, native cherries)
  • Fresh water: Clean water for drinking, irrigation, and industry
  • Medicinal plants: Traditional medicines and pharmaceutical resources
  • Fibre: Materials for textiles, crafts, and construction

TAS Example: The Tasmanian forestry industry provides timber products valued at over $1 billion annually, while supporting native forests with sustainable management practices.

Regulating Services

Benefits from ecosystem processes that regulate the environment:

  • Climate regulation: Carbon sequestration in forests, peatlands, and soils
  • Flood control: Wetlands and vegetation absorb floodwaters
  • Erosion control: Vegetation stabilizes soil and prevents erosion
  • Water purification: Wetlands filter pollutants and sediments
  • Pollination: Native bees, birds, and insects pollinate crops and native plants
  • Air quality: Plants filter air pollutants

TAS Example: The Tarkine rainforest stores an estimated 200 million tonnes of carbon, making it a critical climate regulation service.

Supporting Services

These underpin all other ecosystem services:

  • Soil formation: Weathering and decomposition create soils
  • Nutrient cycling: Decomposition and biological processes cycle nutrients
  • Primary production: Photosynthesis creates organic matter
  • Habitat provision: Ecosystems provide shelter and resources for species
  • Water cycling: Evapotranspiration and infiltration maintain water flows

Cultural Services

Non-material benefits that enrich human experience:

  • Spiritual and cultural values: Connection to Country, Aboriginal heritage
  • Recreation and tourism: Walking tracks, camping, nature appreciation
  • Education and research: Learning opportunities, scientific discovery
  • Aesthetic appreciation: Beauty and inspiration from nature
  • Sense of place: Identity and belonging associated with landscapes

TAS Example: Tourism based on Tasmania's natural values contributes over $2 billion annually to the state economy, supporting regional communities.

Ecosystem Services Summary

Provisioning

Food, water, timber, medicine

Regulating

Climate, flood, erosion, pollination

Supporting

Soil, nutrient cycling, habitat

Cultural

Spiritual, recreation, education, aesthetic

Local Industry Impacts on Tasmanian Ecosystems

Tasmania's industries have shaped the landscape for over 200 years. Understanding their impacts is critical for restoration and management.

Forestry

Impacts

  • Habitat fragmentation: Clearing breaks up continuous habitat
  • Soil compaction: Heavy machinery compacts soil
  • Altered hydrology: Changes to drainage patterns
  • Weed invasion: Disturbance creates opportunities for weeds
  • Loss of old-growth: Reduced habitat for hollow-dependent species

Threatened Species Affected

  • Swift Parrot: Needs old-growth eucalypts for nesting
  • Tasmanian Devil: Habitat loss impacts populations
  • Wedge-tailed Eagle: Nesting trees destroyed
  • Spotted-tail Quoll: Fragmentation reduces range
  • Giant Freshwater Crayfish: Sedimentation from roads and logging

Restoration Actions

  • Native revegetation with appropriate species
  • Habitat corridor creation
  • Protection of old-growth and hollow-bearing trees
  • Soil remediation and erosion control

Agriculture

Impacts

  • Land clearing: Loss of native vegetation
  • Nutrient runoff: Fertilizers, manure entering waterways
  • Weed invasion: Pasture species spreading into bushland
  • Soil degradation: Compaction, erosion, salinity
  • Overgrazing: Damage to native vegetation
  • Pest harborage: Rabbits, foxes, deer

Threatened Species Affected

  • Eastern Barred Bandicoot: Grassland loss
  • Native Grasslands: Less than 5% remain
  • Quolls: Poisoning from baits
  • Wetland species: Water extraction and degradation
  • Soil invertebrates: Chemical use and compaction

Restoration Actions

  • Revegetation of riparian zones and remnant bushland
  • Fencing to exclude stock from sensitive areas
  • Sustainable grazing practices
  • Weed management programs
  • Re-establishment of native grasslands

Mining

Impacts

  • Habitat destruction: Removal of vegetation and soil
  • Water pollution: Acid mine drainage, heavy metals
  • Soil contamination: Chemical residues, tailings
  • Biodiversity loss: Local extinction of species
  • Visual impacts: Scarring of the landscape

Threatened Species Affected

  • Aquatic species: Fish, crayfish, invertebrates
  • Riparian vegetation: Along streams below mines
  • Bat species: Loss of roosting habitat
  • Plants: Direct loss from clearing

Restoration Actions

  • Mine site rehabilitation
  • Water treatment for acid mine drainage
  • Containment of contaminated material
  • Revegetation with appropriate species
  • Long-term monitoring

Tourism

Impacts

  • Trampling: Damage to vegetation and soil
  • Erosion: Track degradation
  • Disturbance: Stress to wildlife
  • Litter: Visual and physical contamination
  • Weed spread: Introduction of seeds on clothing and vehicles
  • Cultural site damage: Vandalism, disturbance

Threatened Species Affected

  • Coastal nesting birds: Hooded Plover, Shearwater
  • Alpine plants: Slow regeneration
  • Aboriginal sites: Middens, rock art
  • Orchids: Trampled by walkers
  • Devils: Road mortality

Restoration Actions

  • Track management and erosion control
  • Visitor education and signage
  • Boardwalks over sensitive areas
  • Fencing around cultural sites
  • Weed hygiene stations

Key Takeaways

  • Tasmania's industries have significant impacts on ecosystems
  • Understanding these impacts is essential for restoration
  • Each industry requires different management approaches
  • Collaboration between industry and conservation is essential
  • Cumulative impacts of multiple industries can be greater than individual effects
